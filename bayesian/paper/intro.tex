\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Classical logic underlies Kolmogorov's probability~\cite{kolmogorov1933}.
The axioms of this probability
specify an arithmetic of chance (non-negativity, normalization,
$\sigma$-additivity). Beneath that arithmetic sits a logic: the events
form a Boolean $\sigma$-algebra, closed under complement and
satisfying excluded middle.  The complement rule,
$P(A^{c}) = 1 - P(A)$, is where the two layers touch.  It is an
arithmetical equation about a logical operation, obeyed by
every assignment of degrees of certainty. In the limiting case
of all-or-nothing probabilities, it becomes
equivalent to the law of excluded middle.

That framework commits to logical axioms that computer science cannot
always commit to.  Many properties of programs (does this computation
halt, does this process reach a given state) are only
\emph{semidecidable}: a procedure confirms the property when it
holds, and no procedure, in general, confirms its failure.  The
natural event algebra for a semidecidable property is a
\emph{frame}, the algebra of open sets of a space, and equivalently
the algebraic semantics of intuitionistic logic.  We show that a
classical, fully resolved (slack-free) probabilistic reading of a
semidecidable property is exactly as hard as solving the halting
problem.  This reading is not exotic: on the space of infinite binary
tapes, the value of the halting event is Chaitin's
$\Omega$~\cite{chaitin1975}, and its slack is the mass of the
topological boundary of the halting event.  Reasoning about the chance that a
program has some property
therefore needs an event logic that is not Boolean. We align the
probability calculus with the logic of the objects it reasons about,
and mechanize the result.

Probability is, as the Cox--Jaynes tradition has it, \emph{extended
logic}~\cite{cox1946,jaynes2003}.  A probability theory has two
layers.  One layer is the event algebra: the underlying set of 
events, and how they combine under the algebraic operations 
of conjunction, disjunction, and negation.
The other layer is the arithmetic: axioms on maps from the event
algebra to numbers, saying which assignments of degrees of certainty
to the events are admissible.  Kolmogorov takes the event algebra to be Boolean, and
his axioms tie the arithmetic to that choice, most visibly in the
complement rule.
The logic of events is in this sense a \emph{parameter} of the
theory, and Kolmogorov's calculus is its value at Boolean logic.
Quantum probability, which re-bases the arithmetic on orthomodular
lattices, is the established precedent for varying
it~\cite{birkhoff-vonneumann1936,gleason1957}.  We compute the value
at intuitionistic logic, whose event algebras are frames: complete
Heyting algebras, the lattices of open sets.  Two facts make the
result a theory of \emph{probability} rather than an arbitrary
grading of events.  In the limit of certainty it returns the logic:
the $\{0,1\}$-valued valuations are exactly the prime-ideal points
of the frame (Section~\ref{sec:hinge}).  And at Boolean logic it
returns the classical theory: complete Boolean algebras are exactly
the frames satisfying excluded middle, and every valuation there
obeys the classical complement rule.  So, the classical theory
embeds as a special case.

The theory that results fixes the arithmetic on the event algebra
and imposes no axiom on complements.
A \emph{valuation} on a
frame $\Omega$ is a monotone, normalized, modular map
$v : \Omega \to \ENNReal$, i.e. such that 
$v(a) + v(b) = v(a \sqcup b) + v(a \sqcap b)$.
The pseudocomplement $\neg a$ satisfies
$a \sqcap \neg a = \bot$ but not, in general, $a \sqcup \neg a =
\top$, so the complement rule degrades to $v(a) + v(\neg a) \le 1$,
with a gap

\[
  \slack(a) \;=\; 1 - v(a) - v(\neg a).
\]

Classically, this is the width of the Dempster--Shafer
belief/plausibility interval~\cite{dempster1967,shafer1976}, 
quantifying ignorance. In our system it instead quantifies the mass of
the part of $a$ that is neither confirmed nor refuted, the probabilistic
image of the semidecidable gap above.
On the opens of a space with measure $\mu$, the slack of an open $U$
is $\mu(\partial U)$, the mass of its topological boundary.
For verification this matters concretely: knowing how likely a
property is to hold no longer tells you how likely it is to fail, and
the gap between the two, the slack, is now also required to answer
that question.

Slack is necessarily submodular, a
consequence of $v$'s own modularity.
It does not, however, determine $v$: two fully modular valuations on
the same frame can share one slack function, a fact we exhibit on an
explicit three-point space.  Weak
excluded middle, $\neg\neg a \sqcup \neg a = \top$, does not repair
this.  Imposed on the whole frame it collapses slack to the
double-negation gap alone, and the fully Boolean case, where even
that gap vanishes, is the worst case for recovery: slack is
identically zero there for every valuation.

The conditional
$v(a \sqcap b)/v(b)$, the product rule, and Bayes symmetry
hold verbatim in the resulting theory, which reduces to
intuitionistic logic in the limit of certainty.
Certain identities vary, however.  The
classical denominator of Bayes' theorem,
$P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) + P(B \mid \neg A)P(\neg A)$, degrades to a
lower bound whose deficit is a conditional slack.  Marginalization is
exact only over partitions. 
The complement trick $P(A) = 1 - P(\neg A)$
degrades to the interval $[v(A), 1 - v(\neg A)]$ of width
$\slack(A)$.  Dempster's rule and Bayesian conditioning, classically
indistinguishable, give different posteriors from the same evidence
unless its slack vanishes.  The value of an event $A$ itself is read
as $P(\mathrm{int}\,A)$, so a nonatomic distribution queried on
intervals still behaves classically: an interval's boundary is two
points, carrying no mass.  Differences arise only at boundaries
carrying mass and at semidecidable hypotheses.

Cox's original theorem required repair after Halpern's
counterexample~\cite{cox1946,halpern1999,vanhorn2003}.  Even with
this repair, the direct translation of Cox's theorem to intuitionistic
logic is \emph{unsatisfiable}.  This is because the sum
rule is irreducible: a disjunction's value is provably not a
function of its disjuncts' values, so modularity must be posited
rather than derived.  Weak excluded middle, an assumption strictly
between intuitionistic and classical logic, does not restore the
reducibility of the sum rule: the counterexample frame itself
satisfies weak excluded middle.

\paragraph{Contributions.}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Two characterizations}
  (Sections~\ref{sec:hinge} and~\ref{sec:ds}).  The complement rule
  holds for \emph{every} valuation iff the frame satisfies excluded
  middle, the exact content of Van Horn's classical negation axiom
  R3~\cite{vanhorn2003}.  Dempster and Bayesian conditioning agree
  iff the evidence has zero slack.

\item \textbf{Structure theorems}
  (Sections~\ref{sec:representation} and~\ref{sec:ds}).  On finite
  frames a valuation is a classical mass function on points.  In
  general it splits into atomic and diffuse parts, and on its
  Boolean core it is a belief function.

\item \textbf{Cox's theorem, dissected} (Section~\ref{sec:cox},
  Appendix~\ref{app:aczel}).  The product-rule half is independent
  of the logic, proved by an Acz\'el/H\"older generator built from
  scratch.  The sum-rule half, a theorem classically, admits no
  derivation here (a five-element counterexample) and must be
  posited.

\item \textbf{Two grounding models} (Section~\ref{sec:bridges}).
  Any classical measure, read on the opens, is a valuation whose
  slack is boundary mass.  A slack-free readout of halting is
  uncomputable, and on Cantor space the value of the halting event
  is Chaitin's $\Omega$.

\item \textbf{The monad dichotomy} (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
  Product valuations are unique on chain products and provably
  non-unique on the powerset frame, where Fubini fails.  The
  countable fragment composes associatively and commutatively.

\item \textbf{Weak excluded middle}
  (Sections~\ref{sec:valuation} and~\ref{sec:bridges}).  Global weak
  excluded middle on the opens of $X$ is exactly extremal
  disconnectedness of $X$, for compact Hausdorff $X$ exactly the
  Stonean spaces.

\item \textbf{A first formal treatment.}
  Everything above is machine-checked in Lean~4 over
  \textsf{mathlib}, to our knowledge the first mechanization of
  non-additive probability over intuitionistic logic.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Mechanization.}
The development is carried out in Lean~4~\cite{moura-ullrich2021} over
\textsf{mathlib}~\cite{mathlib2020}, in roughly $5{,}000$ lines with no
\texttt{sorry}, and is included in full as anonymous supplementary
material.\footnote{Claude (Anthropic) was used to assist with
drafting and debugging the Lean formalization.} Two points are specific
to a proof assistant.  We separate \emph{object-level} classicality,
excluded middle in the frame $\Omega$, from the \emph{meta-level}
classicality \textsf{mathlib} inherits through \texttt{Classical.choice},
track which results use which, and equip the one essential non-Boolean
model with a computable decidability instance.  And the product-rule half of Cox's theorem needs an
Acz\'el/H\"older regraduation absent from \textsf{mathlib}, which we
build from scratch.
