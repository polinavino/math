\section{Valuations Beyond Frames}
\label{sec:lattice}

Definition~\ref{def:frame} fixes the event algebra to be a frame: a
complete lattice in which finite meets distribute over arbitrary
joins $\bigsqcup_{i \in I} a_i$, the operation Heyting implication
$\Rightarrow$ (the right adjoint to $a \sqcap (-)$, so that $a \Rightarrow b$ is the largest $c$ with $a \sqcap c \le b$) is built from.
Definition~\ref{def:valuation}'s four axioms, normalization at
$\bot$ and $\top$, monotonicity, and modularity
$v(a) + v(b) = v(a \sqcup b) + v(a \sqcap b)$, use only the
\emph{binary} join $\sqcup$. None of the four mentions the
\emph{arbitrary} join $\bigsqcup_{i \in I}$. Completeness is used
only where later sections evaluate a valuation on an infinite join,
as in the Scott-continuity results of
Section~\ref{sec:representation}, or build $\Rightarrow$ and $\neg$
from it, as in Section~\ref{sec:hinge}'s slack.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} leaves open how much of the
construction is tied to frames specifically, and whether some
ambient category sends an event algebra to a probability calculus
in general. This section settles both questions for one fragment of
the construction, the sharp/point correspondence of
Section~\ref{sec:hinge}: it isolates the exact algebraic hypothesis
that correspondence needs and shows the hypothesis cannot be
dropped. It does not recover $\Rightarrow$ or $\neg$ without
completeness: the adjoint defining $\Rightarrow$ is built from an
arbitrary join of witnesses, so it is unavailable once completeness
goes, even if only finite joins are missing. The valuations studied
below are negation-free by construction, not frame valuations with
completeness quietly removed.

\paragraph{The functor without completeness.}
A \emph{bounded lattice} is a lattice with a least element $\bot$
and a greatest element $\top$. A \emph{bounded lattice homomorphism}
preserves $\bot$, $\top$, $\sqcup$, and $\sqcap$.
Definition~\ref{def:valuation}'s axioms make sense over any bounded
lattice $\Omega$, complete or not:

\begin{definition}[lattice valuation\leanref{lvaluation}]
\label{def:lvaluation}
A \emph{lattice valuation} on a bounded lattice $\Omega$ is a
monotone function $v : \Omega \to \ENNReal$ with $v(\bot) = 0$,
$v(\top) = 1$, and, for all $a, b \in \Omega$,
$v(a) + v(b) = v(a \sqcup b) + v(a \sqcap b)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lstlisting}
structure LValuation (Ω : Type*) [Lattice Ω] [BoundedOrder Ω] where
  toFun : Ω → ℝ≥0∞
  map_bot' : toFun ⊥ = 0
  map_top' : toFun ⊤ = 1
  mono'    : Monotone toFun
  modular' : ∀ a b, toFun a + toFun b
             = toFun (a ⊔ b) + toFun (a ⊓ b)
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
A lattice valuation on a frame is exactly a valuation in the sense
of Definition~\ref{def:valuation}. The two notions differ only in
how much structure the ambient $\Omega$ carries.

Section~\ref{sec:monad} pulls a valuation back along a frame
homomorphism and checks the assignment is functorial (identity,
composition). That argument pattern-matches on $\sqcup$, $\sqcap$,
$\bot$, $\top$ alone and never touches $\bigsqcup$:

\begin{proposition}[pullback without completeness\leanref{lvaluation-comap}]
\label{prop:lvaluation-comap}
For bounded lattices $\Gamma, \Omega$, a bounded lattice
homomorphism $f : \Gamma \to \Omega$, and a lattice valuation $v$ on
$\Omega$, $v \circ f$ is a lattice valuation on $\Gamma$. This
assignment sends the identity homomorphism to the identity and
composition of homomorphisms to composition of the induced maps.
\end{proposition}

\noindent
$\mathrm{Val}(-)$ is therefore a functor on bounded lattices with
bounded lattice homomorphisms, of which the frame-level functor of
Section~\ref{sec:monad} is the restriction to the subcategory of
frames and frame homomorphisms. Completeness contributes nothing to
functoriality.

\paragraph{The valuation monoid.}
The modularity axiom can be moved from the valuation into its domain.

\begin{definition}[valuation monoid\leanref{vmonoid}]
\label{def:vmonoid}
For a lattice $P$ with least element $\bot$, $V(P)$ is the free
commutative monoid on $P$ (finite formal sums of elements of $P$)
modulo the congruence (equivalence relation compatible with $+$)
generated by
$[a] + [b] \sim [a \sqcup b] + [a \sqcap b]$ for all $a, b$ and
$[\bot] \sim 0$, where $[a]$ is the class of the generator $a$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[universal property\leanref{vmonoid-lift}]
\label{thm:vmonoid}
For every commutative monoid $M$, the maps $f : P \to M$ with
$f(\bot) = 0$ and $f(a) + f(b) = f(a \sqcup b) + f(a \sqcap b)$ are
exactly the composites of $a \mapsto [a]$ with monoid homomorphisms
$V(P) \to M$, and the homomorphism is unique. On a bounded lattice, the
lattice valuations are therefore the monoid homomorphisms
$h : V(P) \to \ENNReal$ with $h([\top]) = 1$ and $a \mapsto h([a])$
monotone.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
So modularity becomes a property of the domain $V(P)$, and normalization
and monotonicity are conditions on the homomorphism.

\begin{theorem}[flags\leanref{vmonoid-flag}]
\label{thm:flag}
In every lattice with a least element, every element of $V(P)$ equals
$[\alpha_1] + \cdots + [\alpha_n]$ for some chain
$\alpha_1 \le \cdots \le \alpha_n$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
The proof inserts one generator at a time into a chain
$c_1 \le \cdots \le c_n$: $[x] + [c_1] = [x \sqcap c_1] + [x \sqcup c_1]$,
and $x \sqcup c_1$ is inserted into $c_2 \le \cdots \le c_n$. Every common
lower bound of $x$ and the chain lies below every element of the result,
which keeps the result a chain. Distributivity is not used. For
distributive lattices the monoid goes back to Tarski and to Horn and
Tarski, who give a normal form of the same kind, a sum of the joins of
the $k$-fold meets of the generators~\cite{tarski1938,horn-tarski1948}.
Coquand and Spitters use it for valuations on distributive
lattices~\cite{coquand-spitters2009}, and Geissinger studies the
universal valuation~\cite{geissinger1973}. On a finite
distributive lattice $\mathrm{Low}(Q)$ the chain is unique up to copies
of $\bot$, and $V(P)$ is cancellative ($x + y = x' + y$ implies
$x = x'$): evaluating against the point
valuations $\delta_q$ gives the number $k(q)$ of elements of the chain
containing $q$, the $j$-th largest element of the chain is
$\{q \mid k(q) \ge j\}$, and $x \mapsto k$ is an injective homomorphism
into $\mathbb{N}^{Q}$. This argument is not formalized. Without
distributivity both can fail.

\begin{proposition}[flags at $M_3$\leanref{vmonoid-m3}]
\label{prop:vmonoid-m3}
In $V(M_3)$, for the lattice of Definition~\ref{def:m3},
$[a] + [o] = [c] + [o]$, and $[a] + [b] = [a] + [c]$ while
$[b] \ne [c]$. So the chain of Theorem~\ref{thm:flag} is not unique, and
$V(M_3)$ is not cancellative.
\end{proposition}

\noindent
Both equations follow from $[x] + [y] = [o]$ for distinct atoms $x, y$.
The map sending $z$ and $c$ to $0$ and $a$, $b$, $o$ to $\infty$ satisfies
the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:vmonoid} and separates $[b]$ from
$[c]$.

\paragraph{Separation needs only distributivity.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp-iff-point} characterizes what a sharp
valuation \emph{is}, a complement-indicator of a prime ideal, but
not whether there are enough of them to distinguish elements of
$\Omega$. That is a separate fact:

\begin{theorem}[sharp valuations separate points\leanref{sharp-separating}]
\label{thm:sharp-separating}
For every frame $\Omega$ and $a, b \in \Omega$ with $a \not\le b$,
there is a sharp valuation $v$ with $v(a) = 1$ and $v(b) = 0$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
The proof runs the same prime-ideal
separation theorem that built the counterexample of
Theorem~\ref{thm:hinge}
(\texttt{DistribLattice.prime\_ideal\_of\_disjoint\_filter\_ideal}),
on the principal filter of $a$ (the elements above $a$) and the
principal ideal of $b$ (the elements below $b$)
rather than on $a \sqcup \neg a$ and $\top$, so it is classical, as in
Table~\ref{tab:classical}. As a corollary, any two
distinct $a, b \in \Omega$ are distinguished by some sharp
valuation, by a classical step: $\Omega$ need not be linear, so $a \ne b$ alone does not
hand back $a \le b$ or $b \le a$, but it forces at least one failure
of $\le$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp-separating} applies in whichever
direction fails. This is the cash-out of the first qualifying
condition of Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}: the certain valuations do
not merely characterize a single point of the locale in isolation,
together they reconstruct the order on $\Omega$. Reconstructing the
order is not the same as reconstructing the full Heyting structure:
$\neg$ and $\Rightarrow$, as just noted, are adjoints built from an
arbitrary join and carry no meaning over a plain distributive
lattice. What this theorem recovers is the order-theoretic core of
the first qualifying condition, not the negation fragment of
intuitionistic logic, which stays frame-specific.

Tracing the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp-separating} shows it
never invokes $\bigsqcup$. The only place frame structure enters is
\texttt{DistribLattice.prime\_ideal\_of\_disjoint\_filter\_ideal}
itself, which needs distributivity and nothing beyond it:

\begin{theorem}[separation over distributive lattices\leanref{lvaluation-separating}]
\label{thm:lvaluation-separating}
For every distributive bounded lattice $\Omega$ and $a, b \in
\Omega$ with $a \not\le b$, there is a sharp lattice valuation $v$
on $\Omega$ with $v(a) = 1$ and $v(b) = 0$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Theorem~\ref{thm:lvaluation-separating}
reproves Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp-separating} with the hypothesis
\texttt{Order.Frame} weakened to \texttt{DistribLattice}, and
nothing else about the argument changes. The condition doing the
work in the sharp/point correspondence is distributivity, and
completeness is not load-bearing for either half of it.

\paragraph{Distributivity is not a proof artifact.}
The natural question is whether
Theorem~\ref{thm:lvaluation-separating}'s hypothesis is a genuine
dividing line or an artifact of routing the argument through a
mathlib lemma named for distributive lattices. It is genuine, and
the failure it guards against is total, not merely a failure of
separation:

\begin{definition}[the diamond\leanref{m3}]
\label{def:m3}
$M_3$ is the five-element lattice with a bottom $z$, a top $o$, and
three pairwise-incomparable atoms (elements directly above the bottom)
$a, b, c$, with $x \sqcup y = o$
and $x \sqcap y = z$ for every two distinct atoms $x, y$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
$M_3$ is the smallest lattice that is not distributive:
$a \sqcap (b \sqcup c) = a \sqcap o = a$, while
$(a \sqcap b) \sqcup (a \sqcap c) = z \sqcup z = z$. It is modular
($x \le z$ implies $x \sqcup (y \sqcap z) = (x \sqcup y) \sqcap z$),
so this failure is specific to distributivity rather than some
coarser defect of the order.

\begin{theorem}[no sharp valuation on the diamond\leanref{m3-no-sharp}]
\label{thm:m3-no-sharp}
No lattice valuation on $M_3$ is sharp.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Modularity applied to each of the three
pairs of distinct atoms forces $v(x) + v(y) = 1$, since
$x \sqcup y = \top$ and $x \sqcap y = \bot$. If $v$ were sharp,
$v(a), v(b), v(c) \in \{0, 1\}$ would have to satisfy all three
pairwise sum-to-one equations at once, and a two-element codomain
cannot support that: fixing any one value propagates through the
other two equations to a contradiction with the third. The collapse
on $M_3$ is therefore not a failure of separation, points that
happen to coincide, but a failure of existence: there is no
all-or-nothing valuation on $M_3$ to separate anything with, and the
first qualifying condition of Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} is
unsatisfiable outright.

\paragraph{Where this leaves the boundary.}
Frames are complete distributive lattices.
Theorem~\ref{thm:lvaluation-separating} shows completeness is not
needed for the sharp/point correspondence's separation half, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:m3-no-sharp} shows distributivity cannot be given
up in exchange. In a classical metatheory, the event algebras on which sharp valuations reconstruct the order,
the order-theoretic core of criterion (1) of
Section~\ref{sec:intro}, are exactly the distributive bounded
lattices, no more and no less. Criterion (2) and
the negation-dependent machinery of
Sections~\ref{sec:hinge}--\ref{sec:bridges}, slack, the complement
bound, the De Morgan gap, are untouched by this section and remain
tied to frames: nothing here shows, or claims, that $\neg$ or
$\Rightarrow$ survives dropping completeness. This bears on the
orthomodular event lattices of quantum probability discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:related}: giving up distributivity, as those
lattices do, does not merely change which valuations exist, it can
remove sharp valuations altogether, so the global construction has
nothing to say about that setting. Section~\ref{sec:quantum-local}
replaces global certain states by certainty within each context.

\paragraph{A second boundary: grading a non-idempotent tensor.}
The event algebras of linear logic are quantales (complete lattices
with an associative multiplication $\otimes$ that distributes over
arbitrary joins), and Section~\ref{sec:related} notes that their tensor
$\otimes$ is not the lattice meet $\sqcap$ this section's valuations
are built from, so Definition~\ref{def:lvaluation}'s modularity
axiom does not even parse for $\otimes$ without first deciding what
to write in $\sqcap$'s place. Granting the substitution
provisionally, to see what it costs, shows the obstruction runs
deeper than a notational mismatch:

\begin{definition}[tensor valuation\leanref{tensor-valuation}]
\label{def:tensor-valuation}
Fix a join-semilattice $\Omega$ (a partial order with binary joins)
and a binary operation $\otimes$ on $\Omega$. A \emph{tensor valuation} for $\otimes$ is a function
$v : \Omega \to \ENNReal$ such that, for all $a, b \in \Omega$,
$v(a) + v(b) = v(a \sqcup b) + v(a \otimes b)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[the transplant collapses\leanref{tensor-collapse}]
\label{thm:tensor-collapse}
For every join-semilattice $\Omega$, binary operation $\otimes$ on
$\Omega$, and tensor valuation $v$ for $\otimes$, if $v(a) < \infty$
then $v(a \otimes a) = v(a)$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Setting $b := a$ in the defining identity and using only
$a \sqcup a = a$, join-idempotence, already gives
$v(a) + v(a) = v(a) + v(a \otimes a)$, hence the claim by
cancellation. No property of $\otimes$ is used: not associativity,
not a unit, not distributivity over $\sqcup$. This is a sharper
failure than Theorem~\ref{thm:m3-no-sharp}'s: $M_3$ shows meet-based
modularity can fail to admit a sharp valuation on one uncooperative
lattice, while Theorem~\ref{thm:tensor-collapse} shows the
$\otimes$-based transplant admits no valuation separating $a$ from
$a \otimes a$ on \emph{any} $\Omega$ and for \emph{any} $\otimes$,
idempotent or not. A non-idempotent $\otimes$, linear logic's whole
point, is exactly what such a valuation could never distinguish from
$\sqcap$'s behavior.

\begin{example}[a concrete witness\leanref{tensor-witness}]
\label{ex:tensor-witness}
Take $\Omega = \mathbb{N}$ with $\sqcup = \max$ and $\otimes = {+}$:
addition is associative and commutative but not idempotent
($1 + 1 = 2 \ne 1$), so the hypothesis of
Theorem~\ref{thm:tensor-collapse} is not vacuous. Substituting
$a = 0$, where the join's unit and the tensor's unit coincide,
forces $v(n) = v(0)$ for every $n$: every tensor valuation for $+$
is constant.
\end{example}

\noindent
This is not a toy chosen merely by analogy with linear logic.
Every quantale, the event algebra Section~\ref{sec:related} attaches
to linear logic, is by definition a complete lattice equipped with
an associative $\otimes$ that need not be idempotent, exactly the
data $(\Omega, \sqcup, \otimes)$ Theorem~\ref{thm:tensor-collapse}
quantifies over, with the quantale's own join playing the role of
$\sqcup$. Example~\ref{ex:tensor-witness} is a quantale itself
($+$ distributes over $\max$), kept only because it is small enough
to check by hand: it fails idempotence for the same reason
$\otimes$ does, no algebraic law identifies two copies of a resource
with one.

\begin{corollary}[grading and discrimination are exclusive\leanref{tensor-dichotomy}]
\label{cor:tensor-dichotomy}
For every join-semilattice $\Omega$, binary operation $\otimes$ on
$\Omega$, and $a \in \Omega$ with $v(a) < \infty$: no $v$ is both a
tensor valuation for $\otimes$ and satisfies
$v(a \otimes a) \ne v(a)$.
\end{corollary}

\noindent
This restates Theorem~\ref{thm:tensor-collapse} as the dichotomy it
really is. A candidate probabilistic semantics for a non-idempotent
$\otimes$ must choose between being graded by
Definition~\ref{def:lvaluation}'s compositional law and
discriminating the one thing a non-idempotent $\otimes$ exists to
discriminate, $a$ from $a \otimes a$. It cannot keep both. The
probabilistic semantics that does exist for linear logic,
probabilistic coherence spaces~\cite{danos-ehrhard2011}, keeps
discrimination: two independent copies of a resource are not scored
as interchangeable with one. Corollary~\ref{cor:tensor-dichotomy}
shows the price of that choice is forced, not chosen: probabilistic
coherence spaces cannot also be graded by this paper's modularity
law, on pain of losing exactly the discrimination they exist to
keep, so they instead evaluate proofs with a classical,
Kolmogorov-style structure over $\otimes$'s type-level behavior,
never a valuation of propositions in Definition~\ref{def:lvaluation}'s
sense. Nothing here is a missed opportunity on either side. The
dichotomy forecloses the combination before any further axiom of
Definition~\ref{def:lvaluation} is even reached. Section~\ref{sec:recipe} replaces these axioms by a construction
that applies to linear logic.

\paragraph{The lattice of logics.}
Ordered by inclusion of their theorems, the logics of this paper lie
below classical logic. Classical logic is the unique maximal consistent
intermediate logic (a logic between intuitionistic and classical
propositional logic), and the unique maximal consistent extension of
quantum logic, because $\{\bot, \top\}$ is a subalgebra of every
nontrivial Heyting algebra and of every nontrivial orthomodular lattice
(a lattice with an orthocomplement $a \mapsto a^{\perp}$ in which
$a \le b$ implies $b = a \sqcup (a^{\perp} \sqcap b)$), so the Boolean
algebras form the least nontrivial variety (a class of algebras defined
by equations) in both cases. Among substructural logics (logics without
some of the structural rules, such as contraction and weakening) it is
one of continuum many maximal consistent logics~\cite{galatos2004}. The three logics leave classical
logic in different directions, and the criteria fail in correspondingly
different ways.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Intuitionistic logic drops excluded middle and keeps
  distributivity. Certain states exist and reconstruct the order
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp-separating}), and the complement rule fails
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:hinge}), so slack appears. Its two parts detect the
  steps from intuitionistic logic to the logic of weak excluded middle
  and from there to classical logic (Theorem~\ref{thm:slack-logics}).
  \item Quantum logic drops distributivity and keeps
  $a \sqcup a^{\perp} = \top$. The complement rule holds, and certain
  states can vanish (Theorems~\ref{thm:m3-no-sharp} and~\ref{thm:ks}),
  so criterion (1) fails for a global calculus.
  \item Linear logic drops the idempotence of $\otimes$. Grading its
  event lattice identifies $a \otimes a$ with $a$
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:tensor-collapse}), so the calculus lives on the webs
  of formulas (the sets of points on which their probabilistic coherence
  spaces are defined, Section~\ref{sec:recipe-linear}), where criterion (3)
  first fails at $!\mathrm{Bool}$ (reusable Booleans,
  Theorem~\ref{thm:bang}).
\end{itemize}
Classical probability is the one case where certain states reconstruct
the order, the complement rule holds, and the grading respects the
tensor.
