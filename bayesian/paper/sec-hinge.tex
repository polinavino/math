\section{Classicality in Theory and Metatheory}
\label{sec:hinge}

Classicality enters this work twice: in the metatheory, the logic
of the proof assistant itself, and in the object theory, the frame's
own logic.

\paragraph{The metatheory.}
Lean's kernel is constructive, and its core library declares
\texttt{Classical.choice} as an axiom, from which excluded middle
follows. A theorem is constructive when its proof does not use this
axiom, which \texttt{\#print axioms} reports. \textsf{mathlib} uses the
axiom throughout. The order on $\mathbb{R}$ and $\ENNReal$ has no
computable \texttt{Decidable} instance, so every library lemma that
case-splits on $x \le y$ for such numbers uses
\texttt{Classical.propDecidable}, and so do the library's finite sums
and finite sets. A theorem that never invokes excluded middle for frame
elements $a \in \Omega$ can therefore still depend on the axiom through
the library. Almost every theorem of this development does. This
dependence is incidental: it comes from the library, not from the
argument. Table~\ref{tab:classical} lists the classical principles the
arguments themselves use, and what a constructive version would
require.

\noindent
The finite combinatorial results ($M_3$, the pentagon, the perfect-graph
criterion, the Kochen--Specker set, and the multiplicative--additive
formulas) need none of these principles. The Cox model of
Section~\ref{sec:cox} is given an explicit computable \texttt{Decidable}
instance.

\paragraph{Object-level classicality: the R3 axiom.}
Van Horn's repaired Cox theorem~\cite{vanhorn2003} axiomatizes
plausibility over classical propositions.  Its negation axiom, called
\emph{R3}, posits that the plausibility of $\neg A$ is a fixed
non-increasing function $S$ of the plausibility of $A$.  Consistency
forces the involution $S \circ S = \mathrm{id}$, and therefore the
complement rule $p \mapsto 1 - p$.  We show that the 
involutive negation \emph{is}
double-negation elimination, so R3 is where classical logic enters
every known Cox-style derivation.  To do this, we first 
isolate the exact content of
R3's conclusion as a predicate on valuations, then prove equivalence
to excluded middle.

That predicate is \emph{classical negation}: a valuation $v$ has
classical negation if $v(a) + v(\neg a) = 1$ for every $a \in \Omega$.

\begin{theorem}[the R3 characterization\leanref{hinge}]
\label{thm:hinge}
For every frame $\Omega$, every valuation on $\Omega$ has a classical
negation \emph{iff} $a \sqcup \neg a = \top$ for every
$a \in \Omega$.
\end{theorem}

The forward direction is a
calculation.  The content is the converse: wherever excluded
middle fails, a valuation with genuine slack must be
\emph{constructed}.  Suppose $a \sqcup \neg a \neq \top$.

\begin{definition}[ideal, prime ideal]
An \emph{ideal} $J \subseteq \Omega$ is a downward closed set which is also closed under finite joins. An ideal $J$ is \emph{prime} if $\top \notin J$ and $a \sqcap b \in J$ implies $a \in J$ or $b \in J$.
\end{definition}

The prime ideal separation theorem
(\textsf{mathlib}'s \texttt{DistribLattice.\allowbreak prime\_ideal\_of\_%
\allowbreak disjoint\_\allowbreak filter\_ideal}) yields a prime ideal $J$ containing
$a \sqcup \neg a$ but not $\top$.  The indicator of $J$'s
complement,
\[
  v(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x \in J \\ 1 & x \notin J, \end{cases}
\]
is a valuation.  Modularity in the only nontrivial case ($x, y
\notin J$) is \emph{exactly} primeness, $x \sqcap y \in J
\Rightarrow x \in J \vee y \in J$.  And this valuation assigns
$v(a) + v(\neg a) = 0 \neq 1$.

\paragraph{Sharp valuations are the points of the locale.}
The construction above is one half of an exact
correspondence.  Call $v$ \emph{sharp} if it is $\{0,1\}$-valued, a
state of full certainty.  Sharpness characterizes exactly the
prime-ideal indicators:

\begin{theorem}[sharp valuations are points\leanref{sharp-iff-point}]
\label{thm:sharp-iff-point}
For every valuation $v$ on a frame $\Omega$, $v$ is sharp iff $v$ is
the complement-indicator of a prime ideal.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
The correspondence is given by two mutually inverse maps: $v \mapsto Z(v) =
\{x \in \Omega : v(x) = 0\}$, which computes the zero-set of a sharp valuation, 
and the map taking
$J$ to its complement-indicator (the valuation of
Theorem~\ref{thm:hinge}'s construction).  $Z(v)$ is prime exactly
because $v$ is sharp, and the complement-indicator of $Z(v)$
recovers $v$ itself.  In locale-theoretic terms, sharp valuations
recover the \emph{points} of the locale.  A prime ideal is
\emph{completely prime} when it is also closed under arbitrary
(not just finite) suprema of its members. 
The finitely prime points 
of Theorem~\ref{thm:sharp-iff-point} sharpen to
the completely prime points exactly where Scott continuity is added,
recovering points of the locale in the sense used
by~\cite{vickers2008,coquand-spitters2009}.

\begin{definition}[directed set, Scott continuity]
A subset $D \subseteq \Omega$ is \emph{directed} if, for any finite
subset $F \subseteq D$, there exists an element $d \in D$ that is an
upper bound of $F$ (taking $F = \emptyset$ shows a directed set is
nonempty).  A
valuation $v$ is \emph{Scott-continuous} if
$v(\bigsqcup D) = \sup_{a \in D} v(a)$ for every directed $D$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Scott-continuous sharp valuations\leanref{sharp-scott-iff-completelyprimepoint}]
For every valuation $v$ on a frame $\Omega$, $v$ is sharp and
Scott-continuous iff $v$ is the complement-indicator of a completely
prime ideal.
\end{theorem}

\noindent  The characterization and the two
correspondences together locate the object-level classicality
precisely: whenever
excluded middle fails at some $a$, the failure occurs at a
point of the locale.

\paragraph{Finite frames without choice.}
The prime ideal theorem is a choice principle. Without it, a frame can
have no points at all~\cite{johnstone1982}, and then it has no sharp
valuations, so the converse of Theorem~\ref{thm:hinge} and the
statement that sharp valuations reconstruct the logic (the first
criterion of Section~\ref{sec:intro}) hold only in a classical
metatheory. On a finite distributive lattice with decidable order the
needed prime filter is found by search: an element below $a$ and not
below $b$ that is minimal for the size of its downset is join-prime, and
the indicator of its principal filter is a sharp valuation. This proves
the finite separation theorem and the finite form of the converse of
Theorem~\ref{thm:hinge} without
\texttt{Classical.choice}\leanref{exists-sharp-separating-fin}\leanref{em-of-sharp-complement-fin},
for $\mathbb{N}$-valued valuations. In a constructive metatheory the
first criterion therefore holds for finite event algebras and for frames
with enough points, and not in general.

\paragraph{A development in Rocq.}
Rocq's kernel, like Lean's, does not assume excluded middle or choice,
so the two differ in their libraries more than in their logic. The
differences would be the following.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Finite results.} The Mathematical Components
  library~\cite{mathcomp} works with types with decidable equality and
  finite types, and avoids classical axioms in its algebraic core.
  The finite theorems of this paper (the finite representation, the
  frame recipe, $M_3$, the pentagon, the perfect-graph criterion, and
  the Kochen--Specker set) would be proved there without classical
  axioms and could be extracted to programs.
\item \emph{Values.} $\ENNReal$ is a classical object. A constructive
  development would use rational values for finite results and lower
  reals, approximable from below, for valuations in
  general~\cite{vickers2008,coquand-spitters2009}. The value of the
  halting event, Chaitin's $\Omega$, is a lower real of this kind: it is
  approximated from below by the halting programs found so far, and no
  better.
\item \emph{Analysis.} MathComp-Analysis~\cite{mathcomp-analysis}
  assumes classical axioms, as \textsf{mathlib} does. The completeness
  theorem for rational points is the Fourier--Motzkin argument above,
  which carries over directly. Completeness over real points would
  require the constructive analysis of Bishop's school, with
  locatedness hypotheses for hyperplane separation. The barycenter
  theorem for infinitely many events requires compactness of
  $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}$, which constructively is the fan theorem and is
  not provable in Rocq's logic without an additional axiom.
\item \emph{Computability.} Without excluded middle the sharp classical
  readout cannot be defined. The uncomputability statement would be
  phrased synthetically~\cite{forster2019}: no function from codes to
  Booleans decides halting, which requires an axiom such as Church's
  thesis to state as a negative result about all functions.
\item \emph{Points.} The separation results would be stated for frames
  with enough points, or for finite lattices as above.
\end{itemize}
Lean was chosen for \textsf{mathlib}'s measure theory, order theory and
topology, which the infinite results use. The cost is that constructive
content is a property to be checked theorem by theorem with
\texttt{\#print axioms}, rather than a default.
